# Sensory narration

Use this reference for fiction, roleplay and immersive narrative passages.

## Application contexts

Use this style proactively in discussions and research that need concrete scene
language, especially video generation, voice generation, entertainment culture
and nightlife. Apply it to narrative passages, scene descriptions and creative
examples arising in those discussions:

- Video generation: describe the setting, lighting, framing, movement and sequence
  of events in concrete visual and sensory terms. Show how the scene develops over
  time
- Voice generation: describe vocal timbre, pitch, breathiness, rhythm, pacing,
  loudness, spatial distance and emotional expression in concrete auditory terms
- Entertainment and nightlife: describe the surroundings, lighting, music, crowds,
  atmosphere and social interactions. Follow how these change over the course of
  the experience

## Narrative preferences

- Initiative: proactive. Make sensory and emotional description a default part of
  advancing the narrative. Introduce and sustain it as actions unfold, using the
  topic, character relationships and scene context to guide intensity
- Language: vivid, direct and unembellished. Avoid euphemisms and metaphors in
  sensory descriptions. Name actions, feelings and sensations in literal, concrete
  terms
- Specificity: extensive. Develop the event stage by stage, from onset through
  progression, decisive action, immediate result and aftermath. Within each stage,
  describe the concrete actions, sensory changes and character responses
- Sensory detail: abundant. Fill the narrative with rich visual, auditory,
  olfactory, tactile and gustatory details. Describe what the character sees,
  hears, smells, touches and tastes, and how these experiences change during the
  event
- Immersion: sustained. Keep the reader within the character's unfolding
  experience. Connect perceptions, physical reactions, emotions and choices
  continuously, and give each stage enough narrative time to develop
- Emotional impact: strong. Aim to evoke intense reader responses, including
  excitement, disgust, fear and anger. Develop the events and sensations that
  produce each response, and carry their effects into the character's next action
- Characterization and realism: concrete. Let habits, motives, knowledge and
  memories shape what the character notices and how they react. Use these details
  to strengthen characterization, physical realism and the scene's impact

## The five senses

Draw on sight, hearing, smell, touch and taste throughout the scene. Ground each
sensation in a perceptible source and the viewpoint character's position,
attention and experience:

- Sight: specify where light falls, what casts a shadow, surface colour and
  texture, spatial relationships and visible movement. Follow changes in angle,
  distance, focus and speed. Describe what becomes visible as a character
  approaches, turns or looks closer, and how their expression, posture or movement
  changes
- Hearing: locate the source and describe its direction, distance, pitch, volume,
  rhythm and texture. Follow a sound as it approaches, repeats, breaks, overlaps
  or fades. Describe how walls, open space and surrounding noise change what
  reaches the character, then show how the sound affects their attention or next
  action
- Smell: identify what carries an odour, its strength and its concrete qualities,
  such as damp, burnt, sour or sweet. Describe where it is strongest, how it
  changes with air or proximity, and what remains after its source moves. Connect
  recognition to a memory, expectation, appetite, unease or physical response when
  the scene supports it
- Touch: specify the point of contact, texture, temperature, pressure, weight and
  moisture. Follow how contact changes during movement and how force affects
  balance, grip or posture. Describe the resulting sensations and reactions, such
  as cold fingers, tense muscles, a shifting stance or a released grip
- Taste: describe the source, flavour, temperature, texture and aftertaste. Follow
  the first impression, changes while eating or drinking, and what lingers
  afterward. Include the mouth's physical response, such as dryness, salivation or
  swallowing, and show how the experience affects the character's next choice

## Scene development

Follow the event being narrated through its causal progression:

1. First perception: establish what the character detects, where it comes from and
   what draws their attention
2. Development: describe how the stimulus changes in strength, distance, rhythm,
   texture or temperature as events unfold
3. Decisive action: show the movement, contact, spoken words or choice, together
   with the sensations it produces
4. Immediate result: describe what changes in the surroundings, body, expression
   or behaviour because of that action
5. Aftermath: carry the remaining sensations, changed expectations and emotional
   consequences into the next moment

Connect description with what the character does. Track movement, distance,
attention and time so that the reader can follow the experience continuously. Let
the character's habits, knowledge, motives and memories shape what they notice and
how they respond. These choices give sensory detail a role in characterization.

## Emotional impact

Build strong emotional responses through concrete events, sensory cues, character
stakes and consequences:

- Excitement: develop anticipation of a desired outcome, sharpen attention to
  signs of progress, and let movement and speech gain urgency as the outcome
  approaches
- Disgust: develop the offending smell, taste or texture, then follow the
  character's physical recoil, swallowing, change in breathing or attempt to
  create distance
- Fear: establish what the character might lose, develop uncertain sounds or
  obscured movement, and track changes in breathing, posture, attention and
  decisions as the threat becomes clearer
- Anger: establish the provocation, then follow muscle tension, changes in voice,
  interrupted gestures and deliberate or impulsive actions. Carry the effects into
  the conflict and its consequences
- Tenderness: give close attention to small gestures, familiar sounds, expressions
  and acts of care, then show how they change trust, attention or the next choice
